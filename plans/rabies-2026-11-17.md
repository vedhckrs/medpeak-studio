## What the sources say (read today)

**WHO, Rabies fact sheet (17 September 2026)**
- "Dog bites and scratches cause 99% of the human rabies cases."
- Spread by saliva, "usually through bites, scratches, or direct contact with mucosa (e.g. eyes,
  mouth, or open wounds)."
- "The incubation period for rabies is typically 2–3 months but may vary from one week to one year."
- "As the virus moves to the central nervous system", the disease becomes "progressive and fatal".
- "Once clinical symptoms appear, rabies is virtually 100% fatal."
- "Rabies deaths are preventable with prompt post exposure prophylaxis (PEP) by stopping the virus
  from reaching the central nervous system."
- What to do: "immediately and always wash the wound with water and soap for at least 15 minutes and
  seek medical attention." Then "a course of human rabies vaccine and, when indicated, rabies
  immunoglobulins".
- "40% being children under 15."

**CDC, About Rabies (27 January 2026)**
- "The rabies virus must travel to the brain before it can cause symptoms."
- Treatment after exposure is "nearly 100% effective if you get it after exposure".

## Shape: the clock (a race), with a short Dr. Mira scene

A race between the virus and the vaccine, inside one arm. The viewer watches the clock, so the
lesson ("go today") is felt, not told.

**Hooks** (pick one; A is in my plan):
- **A** "You can get rabies from one bite."
- B "Get rabies, and your brain is next."
- C "What happens if you get rabies? A race."

**Open question (by 7 s):** "So can you still stop it?"
**Answer (last quarter):** "Yes, you can stop it, but only before symptoms start. Wash, then go today."

## The story

1. **First frame, the arm from inside:** a long glowing nerve runs from a small scratch on the hand
   all the way up and off the top of the picture, where a sign says BRAIN. A virus at the scratch
   looks up the road and grins. Hook.
2. **Rewind, the bite:** a boy pets a street dog; it nips his hand. We see only his face and his
   hand, a small scratch, no blood. Saliva carries the virus in.
3. **The race begins:** the virus walks slowly up the nerve. A clock in the corner ticks over days,
   then weeks. "Usually two to three months", and a red note: "sometimes one week".
4. **Home (the mum, first time on screen):** "It's just a little scratch, Mum." Her answer is the
   first step, straight from WHO: wash it with soap and water, for 15 minutes.
5. **The clinic, Dr. Mira, about 10 seconds:** wash the wound at once, then come straight to a
   doctor. A course of vaccine, and sometimes an injection into the wound, stops the virus before it
   reaches the brain. The twist, said gently: once symptoms start, nothing can stop it.
6. **Back in the arm:** the vaccine sends defenders. They build a wall across the nerve, halfway up.
   The virus hits the wall. Loop to the hook: "One bite, no rabies. Not this time."

**Dr. Mira:** yes. This topic needs a trusted adult saying exactly what to do; that is the case the
plan keeps her for.

## What I will be careful about

- Every first-aid word copied from WHO, never from memory. No dose, no number of injections (countries
  differ; "the doctor decides").
- "Almost 100% fatal once symptoms start" is said once, calmly, by Dr. Mira, followed straight away by
  "and almost 100% preventable if you act in time".
- Not anti-dog: the last line can say that vaccinating dogs is how countries end rabies (WHO).

## The idea in one line

Sam is bitten on the ankle on a trail. Inside his leg, a gang of three **venom drops** bursts out of the two fang holes and sets
off on a journey through his body, each one heading for its own damage. Outside, a friend shouts the old myths ("Cut it! Suck it!
Tie it!") and the venom gang CHEERS for every one of them. Then Sam does the right things, and at the hospital the **antivenom**
arrives through the drip and knocks the gang out.

- **Story shape: `journey`** (the venom travels; Dr. Mira at the end). The last four: clock, fight, journey (creatine, a while
  ago), crew. The look is new: a forest trail, and inside the leg.
- **About 48 s**, then Joey's end card (now played automatically for every video).
- **Places, one colour each** (the rule from fasting): the trail (warm green, day), inside the leg (deep red-brown tissue, the two
  fang holes as a big shaft of light from above), the hospital (clean pale blue), the nerve and the blood shown as big simple
  pictures (a nerve cable, a red river), not small diagrams.

(Lines will be cut to fit about 48 s; the checker will time them.)

Lines written with care:
- **B1-B3** are WHO's own list: venom can cause "paralysis that may prevent breathing", "bleeding disorders", and "tissue damage". We
  say what the venom CAN do; not every snake does all three, and the description says so.
- **C3**: word for word from the NHS "Don't" list ("do not try to suck or cut the venom out of the bite", "do not tie anything
  tightly round the part of the body where the bite is"). No other first aid is shown or hinted at: no bandage method, no ice, no
  herbs.
- **D1-D2**: from the NHS "Do" list ("stay calm", "keep the part of your body that was bitten as still as you can", "take off any
  jewellery ... in case the skin swells", call the emergency number). We do NOT show the venom slowing down because he keeps still:
  the only study I found on that (Sutherland 1979, in monkeys) found that keeping still alone did not delay the venom, so the video
  makes no claim about why.
- **E2**: WHO: antivenoms are "the most effective treatment to prevent or reverse most of the venomous effects of snake bites";
  Gutierrez 2017: antivenom is "the only specific treatment". Given through a drip (NHS).
- **E3**: "dry bites": a venomous snake can bite without injecting venom (Naik 2017, a review). No percentage is given, because the
  sources do not agree on one.

## Care

- First aid ONLY from the NHS list; the description lists it in full. No myths shown as working.
- No numbers of deaths in the video itself (they are in the description: WHO, 81,410 to 137,880 a year), so it does not frighten;
  the tone stays "do the right things and you will be treated".
- Global audience: "call emergency services", not one country's number.

## Sources (every quote read on 2026-11-22)

- **who-snakebite** WHO. Snakebite envenoming (fact sheet). https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/snakebite-envenoming.
  "An estimated 5.4 million people worldwide are bitten by snakes each year"; "Around 81 410 to 137 880 people die each year";
  "Bites by venomous snakes can cause paralysis that may prevent breathing, bleeding disorders that can lead to a fatal haemorrhage,
  irreversible kidney failure and tissue damage"; "High-quality snake antivenoms are the most effective treatment to prevent or
  reverse most of the venomous effects of snake bites"; "Most deaths and serious consequences of snake bites are entirely
  preventable by making safe and effective antivenoms more widely available".
- **nhs-snakebite** NHS. Snake bites. https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/snake-bites/ (read in the browser). Do: "stay calm", "keep the
  part of your body that was bitten as still as you can", "take off any jewellery and loosen clothes near the bite, in case the skin
  swells". Don't: "do not try to suck or cut the venom (toxin) out of the bite", "do not tie anything tightly round the part of the
  body where the bite is", "do not go near the snake, or try to catch or kill it". "you should get all snake bites checked as soon as
  possible"; antivenom "is given through a thin tube into a vein, called a drip".
- **gutierrez-2017** Gutierrez JM et al. Snakebite envenoming. Nat Rev Dis Primers 2017;3:17063. PubMed 28905944. "Intravenous
  administration of antivenom is the only specific treatment to counteract envenoming."
- **naik-2017** Naik BS. "Dry bite" in venomous snakes: a review. Toxicon 2017;133:63-67. PubMed 28456535. "A good percentage of
  venomous snake bites in humans do occur without venom injection."

Read but not used: Sutherland 1979 (PubMed 84206; monkeys; pressure plus keeping still delayed venom, keeping still alone did not:
the reason we make no "keeping still slows it" claim); Juckett 2002 (PubMed 11996419; a US review that still allows a suction device
in the first minutes: it disagrees with the NHS, so we follow the NHS).

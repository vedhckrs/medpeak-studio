## The idea in one line

Grandpa pushes his plate away at 8 p.m. for a 16-hour fast, and his **Stomach** swears it will scream
within four hours. A big clock counts the hours while his body changes fuel: the **Liver's** sugar store
runs dry, a new character, **Lardo the fat cell**, takes over, and the **Brain** gets a new fuel. The
end has a surprise: the hunger was never the stomach's idea.

- **Story shape: `clock`** (one body over time, the hours on screen). Last four: fight (quit-sugar),
  journey (creatine), crew (no-sleep), clock (rabies, the first one on the new look). Fasting IS a clock,
  so the shape fits; the look is new (a night living room, not a lab).
- **The clock on screen is Grandpa's big wall clock** with a red "FAST" ring that fills: 0 h, 4 h,
  12 h, 16 h. Each jump cuts to the next round.
- **About 48 s**, then Joey's CTA end card.
- **Places (4):** Grandpa's living room at night (new), the stomach (whole organ, new frame), the liver

Lines written with care:
- **B2-B3**: a small pilot study (6 people) fasting 24 hours: ghrelin, the hunger hormone, rose and fell
  "at the timepoints of customary meals", and went down overall. "Hunger comes in waves" is the study's
  finding in plain words; we say nothing about minutes.
- **C2**: Anton 2018 puts the switch "typically beyond 12 hours" after the last meal, so "about twelve
  hours", never a fixed hour. It varies from person to person.
- **E2-E3**: Trepanowski 2017 (100 adults with obesity, 1 year): alternate-day fasting lost 6.0% and daily
  calorie cutting 5.3%, "did not produce superior ... weight loss". The TREAT trial (Lowe 2020) found the
  same for 16:8 eating. So fasting is one way to eat less, not a magic one.
- **F1-F2**: word for word from Johns Hopkins Medicine (who shouldn't try it, and two to four weeks to get
  used to it). F2 is on screen as a tag too, held long enough to read.
- **G2**: Natalucci's conclusion: "it is the brain that is primarily involved in the regulation of meal
  initiation." That is the surprise; it is said as "starts in the brain", nothing more.
- **Left out on purpose**: growth hormone and multi-day fasts (the famous numbers come from 5-day lab
  fasts; Johns Hopkins says longer fasts "may be dangerous"), "autophagy" (mostly animal studies),
  detox, "resets your body".

## Care (the topic bank's rule: not safe for everyone, say so)

- **Who should not fast** is said in the video (F2) and in the description, from Johns Hopkins Medicine.
- **No "melts fat", detox, "resets", "burns X kg".** E1 says "melts fat" only as Grandpa's question,
  and the answer says no.
- **No long fasts**: the video is a 16-hour overnight fast, the kind the studies and Johns Hopkins
  describe. Nothing about fasting for days.
- **Conflicts, for you to know**: Trepanowski's last author wrote an every-other-day diet book (stated in
  the paper), and the trial still found fasting was NO better, so the line does not lean his way. TREAT's
  authors had ties to a ketone company (stated); it found no extra weight loss either.

## Sources (every quote read on 2026-11-22)

- **hopkins-if** Johns Hopkins Medicine. Intermittent Fasting: What Is It, and How Does It Work?
  https://www.hopkinsmedicine.org/health/wellness-and-prevention/intermittent-fasting-what-is-it-and-how-does-it-work
  (read in the browser; the page refuses scripts). "Who shouldn't try intermittent fasting? Children and
  teens under age 18. Women who are pregnant or breastfeeding. People with type 1 diabetes who take insulin.
  ... Those with a history of eating disorders."; "it can take two to four weeks before the body becomes
  accustomed to intermittent fasting. You might feel hungry or cranky while you're getting used to the new
  routine."; "after hours without food, the body exhausts its sugar stores and starts burning fat."
- **anton-2018** Anton SD et al. Flipping the Metabolic Switch: Understanding and Applying the Health
  Benefits of Fasting. Obesity 2018;26:254-68. PubMed 29086496. "the metabolic switch: the point of
  negative energy balance at which liver glycogen stores are depleted and fatty acids are mobilized
  (typically beyond 12 hours after cessation of food intake)"; "the metabolic switch from glucose to fatty
  acid-derived ketones". No conflicts.
- **natalucci-2005** Natalucci G et al. Spontaneous 24-h ghrelin secretion pattern in fasting subjects:
  maintenance of a meal-related pattern. Eur J Endocrinol 2005;152:845-50. PubMed 15941923. Six healthy
  volunteers, 24-hour fast: "An increase and spontaneous decrease in ghrelin were seen at the timepoints of
  customary meals"; "An overall decrease in ghrelin levels was observed"; "it is the brain that is
  primarily involved in the regulation of meal initiation."
- **trepanowski-2017** Trepanowski JF et al. Effect of Alternate-Day Fasting on Weight Loss, Weight
  Maintenance, and Cardioprotection Among Metabolically Healthy Obese Adults. JAMA Intern Med
  2017;177:930-8. PubMed 28459931. 100 adults, 1 year; month 12: -6.0% vs -5.3%; "Alternate-day fasting
  did not produce superior adherence, weight loss, weight maintenance, or cardioprotection vs daily
  calorie restriction."
- **lowe-2020** (description only) Lowe DA et al. Effects of Time-Restricted Eating on Weight Loss ... The
  TREAT Randomized Clinical Trial. JAMA Intern Med 2020;180:1491-9. PubMed 32986097. 16:8 eating "is not
  more effective in weight loss than eating throughout the day."

Read but not used: Ho 1988 (PubMed 3127426, growth hormone in a 5-day fast; left out, see care);
Cahill 2006 (PubMed 16848698, a memoir-review with no figure to quote).
